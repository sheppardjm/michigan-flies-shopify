# Water Temperature Drives Michigan's Fly Calendar

Michigan's river fly selection reduces to three overlapping calendars, and water temperature (not the date) is the variable that moves all three. The insect calendar runs early black stoneflies (February–April) → Hendrickson and Little Black Caddis (late April–mid May) → Sulphur, March Brown, Gray Drake (mid May–June) → Brown Drake (early June) → Hex (**peak June 21–July 7 on the Au Sable/Manistee**, 2–3 weeks later in the Upper Peninsula) → Isonychia, Trico, terrestrials (July–September) → White Fly (mid August–September) → fall Blue-Winged Olives; only Hex has a peer-reviewed thermal threshold (**subimagos do not emerge until water reaches 20 °C**, after roughly 1,800–2,030 degree-days above 10 °C), while Hendrickson's 50–55 °F and other cues are angler entomology, and a five-year Hillsdale College study found **calendar date predicts Michigan caddis flight better than water temperature** ([Animal Diversity Web](https://animaldiversity.org/accounts/Hexagenia_limbata/); [Flannagan & Heise 1987](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/1467310); [Houghton 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26339996/)). The egg calendar is nearly continuous from late August to late May: Chinook and coho eggs (7–8 mm, orange) September–October, brown and brook trout eggs October–November, steelhead eggs (translucent yellow-orange) March–April, walleye eggs mid-April, and **white sucker eggs (2–3 mm, pale yellow) peaking around May 10**, which is exactly why pale-yellow "sucker spawn" is a spring fly ([Michigan DNR](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/steelhead); [Manny et al. 2010](https://jamieschmale.ca/walleye/2010_Manny.pdf)). The forage calendar splits species cleanly: resident brown trout over 12 inches are **70–80 % piscivorous in Michigan DNR stomach studies** (small brook trout, sculpins, coho smolts), brook trout and small browns are insect-dominant, steelhead eat eggs, stonefly and Hex nymphs, caddis larvae and fry, Atlantic salmon feed actively in-river (smelt then Hex nymphs), and Chinook, coho and pink salmon strike from aggression rather than hunger ([MDNR RR1855](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1855.pdf); [MDNR RR1759](https://www.michigandnr.com/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1701-RR1800/RR1759.pdf)). For a recommendation engine, the defensible design is a two-layer model: a free air-temperature growing-degree-day grid (gridMET 4 km CC0, NOAA NClimGrid 5 km, USA-NPN 2.5 km with a six-day forecast) calibrated per river against **63 active USGS water-temperature gauges in Michigan**, with the Au Sable (nine gauges) and Manistee (six) as calibration rivers and the ungauged Pere Marquette handled by proxy ([gridMET](https://www.climatologylab.org/gridmet.html); [USGS Water Data APIs](https://api.waterdata.usgs.gov/docs/)). Throughout this report, **[S]** marks university, agency or peer-reviewed findings, **[A]** marks angler, guide or shop recommendations, and **[I]** marks inferences made here.

## The hatch sequence is fixed statewide; only the dates slide

Every Michigan chart, from the Grayling shops to the Upper Peninsula, agrees on the order of emergence and disagrees only on start dates by one to three weeks. The University of Michigan Museum of Zoology's "Aquatic Insects of Michigan" checklist anchors the cast: **at least 127 mayfly species in 42 genera**, roughly **299 caddisfly species in 76 genera**, and a stonefly fauna that includes Allocapnia, Taeniopteryx nivalis, Isoperla, Acroneuria, Paragnetina media ("common in collections") and Pteronarcys dorsata ("widespread in state"), with Hexagenia limbata "widespread throughout the state, in many collections" ([Aquatic Insects of Michigan, Ephemeroptera](https://www.aquaticinsects.org/sp/Ephemeroptera/sp_eom.html); [Trichoptera](https://www.aquaticinsects.org/sp/Trichoptera/sp_tom.html); [Plecoptera](https://www.aquaticinsects.org/sp/Plecoptera/sp_pom.html)). The most detailed text-readable Michigan chart is Trails to Trout's, calibrated to the Au Sable/Manistee "northern rivers" baseline; Superior Flies publishes the same framework recalibrated for the U.P., and Matt Supinski's Gray Drake Lodge chart is the most detailed for the Muskegon tailwater ([Trails to Trout](https://www.trailstotrout.com/resources/michigan-hatch-chart/); [Superior Flies](https://www.superior-flies.com/hatch-chart/); [Gray Drake Lodge](http://graydrakelodgeandoutfitters.blogspot.com/2013/05/new-muskegon-river-hatch-cycle-chart.html)). The Trails to Trout and Superior Flies charts share a row structure and should be treated as one chart with two regional calibrations, not two independent confirmations [I].

Two scientific results should shape how the engine treats this table. First, the temperature-gated hatches are the mayflies: Hexagenia's 20 °C floor is documented, and the degree-day budgets of roughly **1,848, 2,030 and 1,806 DD above 10 °C** for three Manitoba life-history types explain both the two-year Hex cycle in Michigan's cold groundwater rivers and why river hatches trail lakes ([Flannagan & Heise 1987](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/1467310)). Second, caddis behave differently: Houghton tracked 27 species weekly for five years on a first-order northern Lower Michigan stream and found that **"date was a better predictor of flight periodicity than water temperature"** for nearly all species, with most univoltine at the forested site; his companion study of over 18,000 adults on a large Manistee-basin river describes early-to-mid July as "the seasonal peak of adult caddisfly abundance," with activity peaking one to two hours after sunset ([Houghton 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26339996/); [Houghton 2013](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02705060.2013.780187)). The practical consequence is that fixed calendar windows are defensible for caddis while mayfly windows should float with accumulated heat [I]. A third caution is that no Michigan-specific peer-reviewed emergence dataset exists for any river mayfly other than an unretrieved St. Marys Hex life-cycle paper, so Hendrickson's 50–55 °F, Sulphur's 52–60 °F and the rest are angling-entomology rules of thumb (Arbona, Caucci and Nastasi via Troutnut), not controlled studies ([USGS record](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1000336)).

Stage matters as much as species for pattern recommendations. Michigan sources consistently say to skip dun imitations for Gray Drake and Isonychia (both crawl out to emerge) and fish spinners or nymphs; to fish Sulphur emergers and rusty spinners because trout refuse duns; and to fish Hex nymphs year-round, since trout in prime habitat may see "up to 2,000 nymphs per day during non-hatch periods" ([Hawkins Gray Drake](https://hawkinsoutfitters.com/gray-drake/); [Hallowed Waters Sulphur](https://hallowedwaters.com/2021/05/the-ultimate-sulphur-primer/); [Troutnut Hex](https://www.troutnut.com/hatch/32/mayfly-hexagenia-limbata-hex)). The Spring Wiggler, a yellow-chenille and fox-squirrel Hex nymph, has been a Michigan steelhead fly for over fifty years ([SWMTU](https://swmtu.org/fishing/nymph-fishing-for-steelhead)). Named Michigan patterns tied to specific hatches include Regan's Hendrickson Parachute and Spinner #14, Robert's Yellow Drake #4–18 (Sulphur through Hex), Ed McCoy's Real McCoy Gray Drake Spinner, McCoy's All Day Dun and Boondoggle Hex, Borcher's #10–16 for Mahogany and Iso spinners, an Adams #10–12 tied slim for Gray Drakes, and the Adams itself, born on the Boardman ([Streamside](https://michigan-streamside.com/hendrickson-hatch-dark-light-rising-trout/); [Hawkins patterns](https://hawkinsoutfitters.com/fly-patterns-for-michigan-hatches/); [DIY Boardman](https://diyflyfishing.com/boardman-river-michigan/)).

## Mid-state rivers run two weeks early, the U.P. three weeks late

Two independently printed chart rules give near-identical regional offsets. Trails to Trout instructs users of its northern-river dates to "subtract 1–2 weeks" for mid-state rivers (Muskegon, Pere Marquette), "add 1–2 weeks" for Tip-of-the-Mitt rivers (Sturgeon, Pigeon, Black) and "add 2–4 weeks" for Upper Peninsula rivers (Fox, Carp, Ontonagon); Superior Flies, working from a U.P. baseline, says "Tip-of-the-Mitt subtract 1 week; Northern Rivers subtract 2 weeks; Mid-State Rivers subtract 3 weeks" ([Trails to Trout](https://www.trailstotrout.com/resources/michigan-hatch-chart/); [Superior Flies](https://www.superior-flies.com/hatch-chart/)). The two charts' own rows confirm the lag: **Hex peak June 21–July 7 (N-LP) versus July 5–21 (U.P.)**; Hendrickson peak May 1–14 versus May 14–21; Trico start July 20 versus August 3. Sources attribute the offset to latitude and water temperature, never elevation, and 2026's cold spring ("water temperatures were below normal well into late May") is cited as the kind of year that delays everything ([MI Fly Fishing Hub](https://michiganflyfishinghub.com/michigan-hex-hatch.html)).

The Hex hatch is the best-documented worked example of the gradient, and it also shows that thermal class overrides latitude. Angler-published 2026 peaks run **Muskegon June 8–20** ("the tailwater runs warmer than northern rivers, putting it among the first"), **Pere Marquette June 10–25** (often starting the first week of June), **Au Sable Holy Water June 20–28**, Boardman late June, **Manistee June 25–July 5** (tailwater extends the window), Rifle late June–early July, and Jordan, Betsie, Fox and Two Hearted "2–3 weeks behind the Au Sable and Manistee," with "the last week of June through the Fourth of July weekend" the most consistent statewide window ([MI Fly Fishing Hub](https://michiganflyfishinghub.com/michigan-hex-hatch.html)). The Escanaba's aggregator grid shows Hex July–August, and on the St. Marys the mid-July Hex emergence is what switches Atlantic salmon from baitfish to nymphs ([Fly Shack Escanaba](https://www.flyshack.com/HatchChart.aspx?RiverID=1415); [Rivers North](https://riversnorth.net/atlanticsalmon.html)). Combining the 20 °C threshold with these dates implies Au Sable water crosses roughly 68 °F in the third week of June in a normal year [I].

## Salmonid presence by river follows the DNR's weir calendar

The Michigan DNR's own operations provide the firmest month anchors for migratory species. Steelhead "enter their spawning streams from late October to early May," overwinter, and spawn in spring with "the fall-run fish typically the first to spawn, often in March, followed by the spring run fish in April"; the Little Manistee weir, the state's only steelhead egg source and a mostly wild run, is boarded in mid-March with **egg takes scheduled April 14–22, 2026** ([MDNR Steelhead](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/steelhead); [MDNR 2026 release](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/about/newsroom/releases/2026/04/06/dnr-to-begin-spring-steelhead-egg-collection-on-little-manistee-river-april-14)). Chinook are "usually present in catchable numbers by mid-August," Pacific salmon egg takes run mid-September to late October, and the 2025 Little Manistee Chinook take ran **September 29–October 8** on "another strong run of wild Chinook"; Platte coho eggs were taken **October 16–28, 2025** ([MDNR Chinook](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/chinook-salmon); [Outdoor News](https://www.outdoornews.com/2025/12/16/michigan-dnr-fisheries-staff-collected-more-than-16-million-trout-and-salmon-eggs-this-season/)). Coho runs span "early September to November," with a notable late-October Manistee fishery and St. Joseph fish "as late as Christmas" ([MDNR Coho](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/coho-salmon)). Pink salmon runs "begin in the summer," concentrate in the St. Marys rapids ("the most outstanding pink salmon fishery this side of Alaska") and Lake Superior and northern Huron tributaries, and remain largest in odd years though even-year runs have existed since 1976 ([MDNR Pink](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/pink-salmon); [MDNR RR1893](https://www.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1893.pdf)). Atlantic salmon have one established river fishery, the St. Marys, where the run "typically begins in mid-summer and runs until November when spawning commences," with experimental fall (October–December) returns to the Au Sable, Thunder Bay River and Lexington Harbor and a Torch Lake population ([MDNR Atlantic](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/atlantic-salmon)). Brown trout spawn in tributaries in September–October, brook trout in October–November, and lake trout on shoals in fall with only incidental river entries ([MDNR Brown](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/brown-trout); [MDNR Brook](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/brook-trout); [MDNR Lake trout](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/lake-trout)).

Two structural facts should be encoded rather than left implicit. Skamania summer-run steelhead are an agency program only on the St. Joseph, where Indiana DNR documents returns beginning mid-to-late June and spawning February to mid-March; the Big Manistee below Tippy is called "the most dependable summer-run steelhead fishery in the state" on Indiana-sourced stock, and reports of summer fish in the Grand, Muskegon, PM and Rogue rest on angler media ([Indiana DNR](https://www.in.gov/dnr/fish-and-wildlife/fishing/lake-michigan-fishing/); [Great Lakes Angler](https://www.glangler.com/blogs/articles/michigan-s-summer-run-by-robert-gwizdz)). Lake Superior tributaries run later, with a spring peak in early-to-mid May (the Two Hearted "mid-May, usually") and a fall window compressed to October–November, and they are predominantly wild ([Trout and Steelhead](https://troutandsteelhead.net/lake-superior-steelhead-fishing/); [RiverReports](https://www.riverreports.com/river-intel/topics/great-lakes-steelhead-fishing)). Three trend facts affect how long these windows stay valid. Lake Michigan Chinook were **72 % wild in 2024** with a record 9.2-million wild 2023 year class, and the rivers with significant natural reproduction are the Muskegon, White, Pere Marquette, Little Manistee, Manistee and Betsie, so the planned 2027 stocking cut will not change river presence through at least 2028 ([AOL/Journal Sentinel](https://www.aol.com/news/lake-michigan-fishery-producing-great-105509573.html); [Michigan Sea Grant](https://www.canr.msu.edu/news/fast_facts_on_lake_michigan_salmon_and_trout_msg16_okeefe16); [Ludington Daily News](https://www.shorelinemedia.net/ludington_daily_news/news/chinook-plants-likely-to-be-reduced-beginning-in-2027/article_20122886-2556-44b6-a4b0-b654a3483117.html)). Steelhead are the fragile species: Little Manistee weir counts fell from just over 6,000 (2002) to under 2,000 (2020), the NRC cut the spring bag limit to one fish March 15–May 15 on Bear Creek, the Manistee, PM, Muskegon, Manistique and Carp in 2022, and an April 15, 2026 power failure at the weir killed more than 1,700 adults, leaving 2027 stocking uncertain ([Bridge Michigan](https://bridgemi.com/michigan-environment-watch/steelhead-struggle-some-michigan-waters-will-catch-limit-help/); [Interlochen Public Radio](https://www.interlochenpublicradio.org/ipr-news/2026-04-15/storm-knocks-out-power-to-little-manistee-river-weir-killing-steelhead)). ## Eggs drift ten months a year, and each month has a color

Egg patterns work in Michigan because the supply of drifting eggs is nearly continuous and because most of what drifts is non-viable. The USGS explains that egg color comes from dietary carotenoids and ranges "from pale yellowish-orange to dark reddish-orange," and that unfertilized eggs "lose their bright appearance and turn a milky shade with patches of color," which is the biological basis for veiled Nuke Eggs and cream "dead egg" yarns ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/why-do-salmon-eggs-come-different-colors)). Documented egg diameters give the size ladder: landlocked Chinook mean **7.22 mm (6.36–8.36)**, Atlantic salmon ~5.6 mm, brown trout 4–5 mm, white sucker **3.0–3.1 mm** and walleye **2.0–2.1 mm** as measured in the Detroit River ([Krebs et al. 2018](https://www.alliedacademies.org/articles/landlocked-fall-chinook-salmon-egg-size-is-positively-related-to-hatching-time-10971.html); [Manny et al. 2010](https://jamieschmale.ca/walleye/2010_Manny.pdf); [ICES J. Mar. Sci.](https://academic.oup.com/icesjms/article/69/9/1678/639366)). Coho and pink (6–8 mm) and steelhead (3–7 mm) sizes rest on vendor and aquaculture ranges rather than Great Lakes measurements ([Stone Cold Beads](https://stonecoldbeads.com/bead-fishing-basics/); [The Fish Site](https://thefishsite.com/articles/cultured-aquaculture-species-rainbow-trout)).

The spawn calendar below is what makes "sucker spawn is a spring fly" a testable statement rather than lore. The Detroit River study found walleye spawning peaked the week of April 12–19 as water rose from 6.5 to 9.0 °C and **white sucker spawning peaked May 10**, during a four-week rise from 6 to 10 °C between April 26 and May 17; Michigan Sea Grant's West Michigan creek monitoring reports white and longnose suckers responding to a **43.3 °F temperature cue**, and the DNR notes runs "may begin in mid-March in southern Michigan, or as late as early May in the north" ([Manny et al. 2010](https://jamieschmale.ca/walleye/2010_Manny.pdf); [Michigan Sea Grant](https://www.michiganseagrant.org/blog/2025/03/20/spring-brings-spawning-fish-into-west-michigan-streams-and-you-can-help-to-monitor-spawning-runs-in-local-creeks/); [MDNR carp and suckers](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/carp-suckers)). On the Muskegon, guides report "over 100,000 Red Horse Suckers" ascending in May–June with big trout and drop-back steelhead feeding behind the redds ([Betts sucker spawn](https://bettsguideservice.com/michigan-trout-guides-sucker-spawn/)).

Chuck-and-duck is a Michigan invention with no dated primary source. Hawkins says it "was born" on the Manistee, "essentially using lead and a thinner line to get egg and nymph flies to the bottom," and a forum attribution credits Pere Marquette guide Mike Gnatkowski, importer of Slinky Drifters, with rigging shooting line and slinkies so non-casting clients could fish the flies-only water; both are lore ([Hawkins Manistee](https://hawkinsoutfitters.com/manistee-river-steelhead-fishing/); [Michigan Sportsman](https://www.michigan-sportsman.com/threads/the-martin-72-multiplier-a-michigan-legend.123000/)). The representative build from Baldwin Bait & Tackle, Current Works, Nomad Anglers and Trails to Trout is 30 lb backing, about 100 ft of level running line or 20–30 lb Amnesia, a **7–8 ft butt of 12 lb Maxima on small rivers (Betsie, Bear Creek, PM) or 8–12 ft on big rivers (Manistee, Muskegon, Au Sable)**, a sliding #10 snap swivel carrying a slinky or pencil lead buffered by a bead against a #10 barrel swivel, 30–48 in of 6–10 lb tippet to the first fly and 24–36 in to a second fly tied off the bend, lobbed "straight across stream or slightly quartered down" with "one cast to the fish, not several false casts" ([Baldwin Bait & Tackle](https://fishbaldwin.com/pere-marquette-travel-fishing-tips/steves-explanation-of-chuck-n-duck-indicator-rigs-for-migratory-fish/); [Current Works rigging](https://www.current-works.com/fly-fishing-articles/rigging-for-steelhead-salmon/); [Nomad Anglers](https://nomadanglers.com/pages/michigan-steelhead-set-up); [Trails to Trout](https://www.trailstotrout.com/resources/salmon-fishing-tips/)). The indicator rig on a 9.5–11 ft single-hand or switch rod, with a 9 ft "Pere Marquette standard" leader and a sliding float sized to the shot, is now "arguably the most effective" method and "expands the fishable water tenfold" in wood; Skagit swing on 12–14 ft rods and Euro-nymphing with sighters are the other modern options ([Nomad Anglers](https://nomadanglers.com/pages/michigan-steelhead-set-up); [Hawkins](https://hawkinsoutfitters.com/manistee-river-steelhead-fishing/); [Fly Fisherman](https://www.flyfisherman.com/editorial/guide-strategies-for-great-lakes-winter-steelhead/370579)).

The 2026–27 regulations set hard constraints the quiz must respect. An artificial fly is a single hook or two in-line single hooks dressed with thread-attached material, gap **½ inch or less**, nothing above the eye, and "the fly or leader may be weighted, but no weight shall be attached... in a manner that allows the weight to be suspended from or below the hook"; **"a bead is not a fly and is not allowed in flies only gear restricted waters,"** though a bead pegged within 4 inches of a ½-inch single hook is a legal lure elsewhere (newly codified for 2026); on all streams August 1–May 31 single hooks over ½ inch and lures over one ounce are illegal; drop-shotting is illegal on rivers; scented material is illegal on flies-only reaches; and listed salmon rivers (Betsie, Bear Creek, Manistee below Tippy, Big Sable, PM, Little Manistee, White, Muskegon below Croton, Pentwater branches) carry additional single-hook terminal-gear rules August 1–November 15 ([2026 Michigan Fishing Regulations](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/-/media/Project/Websites/dnr/Documents/LED/digests/2026-Michigan-Fishing-Regulations_web_accessible.pdf)). Only three reaches are both flies-only and no-kill year-round: the Au Sable Holy Water (Burton's–Wakeley), the South Branch Chase Bridge–Lower High Banks, and the PM M-37–Gleason's Landing; the North Branch, lower South Branch, Manistee M-72–CCC and Little Manistee Spencer's–Johnson are flies-only with two-fish harvest, and "artificial lures only" gear-restricted reaches (Mio Trophy Water, Wakeley–McMasters, upper Manistee 612–M-72, Pigeon, Black, Escanaba) are a separate flag from the county "special artificial lure" single-hook rules [I]. Chuck-and-duck with weight on the leader above yarn eggs is therefore legal on flies-only water; the same rig with a bead is not.

## Big browns eat fish, steelhead eat eggs and nymphs, Pacific salmon eat nothing

The only Michigan stomach-content science is old DNR work, and it is unambiguous about brown trout. On the North Branch Au Sable (1962–1971), "the diet composition of North Branch Au Sable trout is **70 to 80 % fish**," with small brook trout 30–37 % of the diet in the general-regulation water and 42–61 % in the flies-only water; brown trout are "the most significant natural predator yet identified on the Au Sable in terms of total trout killed" ([MDNR RR1855](https://www2.dnr.state.mi.us/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1801-RR1900/RR1855.pdf)). In the Anna River (U.P.), browns under 11.8 inches "fed primarily on Trichoptera" while larger fish "ate mostly fish": stocked coho smolts (3.6–5.7 in) in May and slimy sculpins June–October, and they "selected salmonids over sculpins to eat, even though sculpins were nine times more abundant"; sculpins were about 90 % of available prey ([MDNR RR1759](https://www.michigandnr.com/publications/pdfs/DNRFishLibrary/ResearchReports/RR1701-RR1800/RR1759.pdf)). This is the scientific justification for Michigan's streamer culture. Brook trout, by DNR description, are "voracious feeders" on mayflies, stoneflies, aquatic and terrestrial insects that "will often feed on whatever is most readily available like zooplankton, crustaceans, worms and fish"; the only quantified terrestrial share is from West Virginia, where terrestrials were **38–47 % of yearly biomass and 51–63 % of energy** consumed by stream brook trout ([MDNR Brook](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/brook-trout); [TAFS 137:224](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article/137/1/224/7888240)). No Michigan study quantifies Hex nymphs, crayfish, scuds, leeches or terrestrials as a share of any resident trout's diet, and the juvenile-steelhead diet paper for the Muskegon (Godby et al. 2007) is paywalled ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1577/M06-077.1)).

For migratory fish the split is between feeders and strikers. Steelhead demonstrably feed in rivers: Michigan guides describe fall fish switching from lake alewife to river shiners, winter fish on sculpins, gobies and darters, and spring fish gorging on Chinook fry along shorelines, stoneflies and eggs, and the St. Marys steelhead flies imitate "the Hexigenia mayfly nymph, certain caddisfly nymphs, stonefly nymphs and fish roe" ([Anchored Outdoors/Feenstra](https://anchoredoutdoors.com/guaranteed-to-catch-more-fish-understanding-baitfish-seasons-with-kevin-feenstra/); [Betts spring](https://bettsguideservice.com/muskegon-river-fishing-report-spring-steelhead/); [Perfect Fly St. Marys](https://perfectflystore.com/your-streams/fly-fishing-the-st-marys-river-michigan/)). No Michigan adult-steelhead stomach study was found; the widely quoted "90 % of winter steelhead have empty stomachs" figure is uncited in the article that carries it ([MidCurrent](https://midcurrent.com/v2/what-do-winter-steelhead-eat-a-guide-to-fly-selection/)). Pacific salmon are the opposite case: the DNR states that on the spawning run salmon "do not feed but will often strike out of aggression," and fisheries biologists attribute strikes to redd defense ([MDNR Atlantic](https://www.michigan.gov/dnr/education/michigan-species/fish-species/atlantic-salmon); [FISHBIO](https://fishbio.com/the-bites-on/)). Atlantic salmon are the exception among salmon: St. Marys guides describe fish arriving in late June on smelt and juvenile salmonids ("stripping large baitfish imitation patterns on sink tips"), switching to insects when "the Hexagenia hatch occurs in mid-July," and a DNR biologist reports Au Sable Atlantics "being caught in January, February, March and April" on streamers and nymphs ([Rivers North](https://riversnorth.net/atlanticsalmon.html); [FishSens](https://www.fishsens.com/st-marys-rivers-atlantic-salmon-among-the-hardest-fighting-fish-in-the-great-lakes/); [Great Lakes Angler](https://www.glangler.com/blogs/articles/au-sable-atlantics-robert-gwizdz)).

Michigan's streamer tradition is itself a data point about what the fish eat. Kelly Galloup developed the Zoo Cougar in the mid-1990s for the Manistee, published "Modern Streamers for Trophy Trout" with Bob Linsenman in 1999, Russ Maddin's Circus Peanut (around 2000) "ushered in the age of articulated streamers," and Tommy Lynch's Drunk & Disorderly was built on the Pere Marquette and Upper Manistee to swim like an injured baitfish, where "several browns over 24 inches" have taken it ([RiverKeeper Flies](https://www.johnkreft.com/galloups-zoo-cougar/); [MidCurrent Circus Peanut](https://midcurrent.com/2025/07/13/video-the-story-behind-russ-maddins-circus-peanut/); [Orvis D&D](https://news.orvis.com/fly-fishing/big-and-meaty-tommy-lynchs-drunk-disorderly-streamer)). Galloup's operating rule is a color rotation of "White, Black, Tan, Olive, Yellow, Chartreuse," and "when in doubt, don't go big, you go small," because big flies trigger aggression while smaller flies feed selective fish ([Wet Fly Swing](https://www.wetflyswing.com/kelly-galloup-on-the-best-streamer-strategies-for-giant-trout/)). A recommendation engine should therefore ask setup (rod weight, line) before recommending streamers, and should present piscivory as the default for brown trout larger than 12 inches while offering hatch-matching for brook trout and small fish [I].

## Free grids and 63 USGS gauges can power the calendar

No published, calibrated air-temperature degree-day model exists for any Michigan hatch; the peer-reviewed literature uses water temperature and species-specific thresholds. Beyond Hexagenia's 10 °C base and ~1,800–2,030 DD budget, the Sweeney/Vannote lineage models stream mayflies as two sequential stages with development "a linear function of streamwater temperature above a lower threshold" and quiescence above a maximum, and a 2020 Stroud model for a baetid found that a day-length-triggered larval quiescence "enhance[s] synchronization of adult emergence" ([Newbold, Sweeney & Vannote 1994](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.2307/1467261); [Kolpas et al. 2020](https://stroudcenter.org/publications/phenological-modeling-parthenogenetic-mayfly-white-clay-creek/)). For the western salmonfly, "peak emergence never occurred if water temperatures were below 8.4 °C" and emergence advanced 2.8 days per degree of spring warming ([Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/ecology-and-evolution/articles/10.3389/fevo.2023.804143/full); [Henry's Fork study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229532226_Effect_of_springtime_water_temperature_on_the_time_of_emergence_and_size_of_Pteronarcys_californica_in_the_Henry's_Fork_catchment_Idaho_USA)). The proxy problem is that Michigan trout rivers are groundwater-buffered: weekly air-to-water regressions achieve about **1.6 °C RMSE at 98 % of 584 USGS stations nationally**, but "ground-water inflows, stream shading, and wind sheltering... reduc[e] the slope of the relationship," so air-GDD to emergence curves will be flatter and lagged on the Au Sable and PM than on air-coupled rivers, and per-river calibration is mandatory ([Mohseni et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245300737_Estimating_Stream_Temperature_from_Air_Temperature_Implications_for_Future_Water_Quality); [air–stream review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/253295075_The_Relationship_Between_Air_Temperature_and_Stream_Temperature)). Plant indicators (lilac, honeysuckle, "popple leaves the size of a quarter" for suckers) are folk phenology with a real mechanism, and USA-NPN's Spring Index lilac/honeysuckle models are themselves gridded heat-accumulation models, so a first-bloom layer is a free secondary cue that will track air-coupled spring hatches (Hendrickson, Grannom) far better than Hex ([USA-NPN Spring Index](http://www.usanpn.org/data/maps/spring); [IJC sucker run](https://ijc.org/en/tracking-sucker-run-how-great-lakes-fish-sustains-food-webs)).

The fish side has better Michigan science than the insect side. Workman, Hayes and Coon modeled 28 radio-tagged Pere Marquette steelhead plus 5,876–10,083 annual St. Joseph fishway passages and found "the probability of movement increased with increasing water temperatures above a movement-threshold water temperature," with a power function fitting best and stream flow adding little; the GLFC companion report puts peak activity above 7 °C and ladder passage above 9 °C on rising or steady temperature ([Workman et al. 2002](https://academic.oup.com/tafs/article/131/3/463/7891291); [Snell, Coon & Hayes](https://www.glfc.org/pubs/pdfs/research/reports/Snell_Coon.pdf)). Fall salmon entry is a cooling threshold (rivers dropping into the 60s °F) plus rain, which is better modeled from USGS water temperature as a first-date-below-threshold than from air GDD [I].

The recommended architecture is two layers over a small grid. Layer one pulls gridMET tmmx/tmmn (or NClimGrid) nightly for a Michigan bounding box, computes per-cell daily GDD from January 1 at three bases (32 °F, 42 °F and 50 °F, optional 86 °F cutoff, simple average method as USA-NPN does), and appends the NPN or NWS forecast for the next six to seven days. Base 50 °F matches the 10 °C literature threshold and suits Hex, Brown Drake, Isonychia and summer caddis; base 32 or 42 °F is needed for Hendrickson, Grannom, early Baetis and stoneflies, which emerge at 45–55 °F water when base-50 air GDD has barely begun accumulating in northern Michigan [I]. Layer two reads the USGS daily collection to compute water degree-days on gauged reaches, supplies absolute triggers, and fits per-river offsets expressed in AGDD (not days) and grouped by thermal regime. Each hatch then stores a calibrated AGDD window per river, fitted from five to ten years of local hatch reports against cell AGDD, rather than a single statewide number; no source says how many seasons are needed for a stable offset, so the team must build that training set itself. Resolution of 2.5–5 km is sufficient because emergence varies more by reach (groundwater, tailwater) than by sub-kilometer air gradients, which is why Daymet's 1 km adds nothing operationally.

The competitive landscape leaves the method open. IdentaFly, FlyFishFinder, Buggin, RiverReports, Current and TroutFishing.app combine static hatch charts with live USGS flow and weather, and none publishes a degree-day method; Troutnut's own view is that "hatch charts are a general reference at best and meaningless at worst" and that a relationship between growing-degree-day requirements and hatch timing is a hypothesis worth testing ([IdentaFly](https://my.identafly.app/); [Buggin](https://www.bugginfishing.com/); [Troutnut forum](https://www.troutnut.com/topic/694/When-does-a-Hatch-happen); [Troutnut, Predicting a hatch](https://www.troutnut.com/topic/1319/Predicting-a-hatch)). Showing "AGDD to date versus this river's calibrated window" beside the live gauge temperature and the DNR gear flag for the chosen reach would be a transparent, defensible differentiator. For the quiz inputs, the mapping is direct: **where** selects the river's regional offset, thermal class, gauge (or proxy) and regulation flags; **when** selects the month slice of the hatch, egg and run calendars adjusted by current AGDD; **target species** selects the feeding model (hatch-matcher, piscivore, egg-and-nymph feeder, aggression striker, in-river feeder for Atlantics); and **setup** (3–5 wt dry, 6–7 wt streamer, 7–8 wt indicator or chuck-and-duck, two-hand swing) filters the pattern table by size and technique.

## Conclusion

The research supports a simple but non-obvious design principle: Michigan's fly calendar should be modeled as three thermal processes rather than one date table. Insect emergence is a heat-accumulation problem gated by water temperature (with caddis as a date-driven exception), egg availability is a spawning-temperature problem that moves from cooling triggers in fall to warming triggers in spring, and forage selection is a fish-size and species problem that barely depends on the date at all. Because the same USGS gauges feed all three, and because the Au Sable and Manistee are among the best-instrumented trout rivers anywhere, the calibration burden is smaller than it looks; the hard part is assembling five to ten years of dated local hatch reports from Gates, Current Works, Gray Drake Lodge and Superior Flies, which are exactly the shops whose charts remain locked in images.

Two honest limits should be disclosed in the product. First, the scientific layer is thin: outside Hexagenia degree-days, Houghton's caddis phenology, the steelhead movement model and two DNR brown trout diet studies, every timing and pattern claim here is angler-sourced, and the report's [S]/[A] tags should carry through to the interface so users can see which recommendations rest on data and which on guide consensus. Second, the migratory baseline is shifting: majority-wild Chinook and declining stocked steelhead mean the DNR Fish Stocking Database and annual weir egg-take releases should be re-pulled each year, and the April 2026 Little Manistee weir kill is a reminder that even the most reliable anchors can move.
